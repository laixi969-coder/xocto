# The GTM Co-Founder

## What it is in one line

A marketing brain for technical founders who don't have a co-founder: who buys, why they'd pay, how to open the conversation.

## Who built it

The GitHub organization `AIDevGTM`, site at gtmcofounder.com.

The README doesn't open with features. It opens with this:

> "If you're a technical founder paying out of your own pocket, putting in the nights and weekends,
> with no funding, no team, no business co-founder, and some days are just plain hard — this is for you.
> Especially for you."

_Read: whoever wrote that has lived it. That's the strongest founder-product fit signal there is,
and putting it above the fold says the author knows it's the real pitch._

## What it actually does

A set of GTM skills fed to an AI coding agent (open source, MIT):

- **Describe your product** → it forces you to answer who this is actually for, turning a vague
  target user into a specific person
- **Describe your features** → it works out why anyone would pay for that, finding the real reason
  to buy rather than a feature list
- **Give it a sales situation** → it gives you an opening structure and the questions to ask
- **Walk the framework** → it produces a list of the traps technical founders usually fall into

**What it deliberately does not do**: it won't decide for you.
It gives you an order of questions and a checklist, not "in your situation, pick A over B."
And that is exactly what customers actually want — which is what caps it.

## What old behavior it replaces

A technical founder finding a business co-founder, or paying a consultant, buying a course, or hanging around a community.

The old behavior is expensive: a co-founder takes equity, consultants bill by the day, and a course
you bought has nobody to answer "but what about my specific situation?" Meanwhile the pain hits
**daily** — his own phrasing is "two in the morning and nobody to talk it through with."

**What you lose in that moment isn't money, it's direction.** That kind of pain is the most real,
because it can't be postponed and it can't be outsourced.

## The call

This product is attempting something genuinely hard: **packaging judgment as a tool.**

GTM is a chain of decisions with no standard answers — who is this feature for, what is it worth,
which channel first. It isn't a process problem, it's a cognition problem. Turning it into a skill
assumes that a good order of questions can substitute for experience. That assumption is probably
half right.

**The transferable rule: precision of emotional entry can substitute for product maturity during a cold start.** Taking the daily top spot almost certainly had less to do with capability than with the fact that "especially for you" landed on a specific group of people. That's a marketing win, not a product win — but early on it counts the same.

The cost is that retention for this kind of product will look ugly. Emotional resonance drives
downloads; it doesn't answer "what should I do in my situation." As soon as users notice they're
getting a generic framework instead of a targeted call, they leave.

## What to watch next

1. **What the site actually sells** — whether anything paid follows the free skills decides whether this is a business or a piece of work
2. **Whether stars are still climbing in three months** — a daily top spot delivers a pulse; only the curve shape shows retention
3. **Whether any user case studies appear** — someone making a GTM decision on it and succeeding is what would prove the assumption

## What you can take from it

**It draws a line worth stealing: the order of the questions and the list of traps can be standardized; which path fits your specific case cannot.** That line is your pricing boundary. Anything on the standardizable side should be given away free; the judgment side is what people actually pay for.

- **What it can do**: question order, which metrics to look at, common traps — those genuinely standardize
- **What it can't do**: choosing the right path in a specific situation. Which is what customers pay for

**Positioning**: look at the opening line. It doesn't say "we provide GTM solutions."
It says "some days are just plain hard." That move is standard in consumer marketing and
almost unheard of in developer products — which is exactly why it won.

## Verdict

**Worth watching.** The product itself may not last long, but the line it draws between
"standardizable" and "requires judgment" is directly useful to anyone trying to productize expertise.
