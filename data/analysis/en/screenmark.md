## What it is in one line

A live "explainer whiteboard" in the macOS menu bar: draw, zoom, highlight, freeze
the frame, and record over whatever screen you are already sharing — plus a
companion iPhone remote — without breaking your presentation flow.

## Who built it

Mustafa Cerit, an independent developer. On the App Store the app appears as
"Screen Mark: Annotation & Draw," with a companion app, ScreenMark Remote
(released 2026-06-09). Team size and company entity: not disclosed.

_Read: a solo-maintained utility that takes the "point at something while you
talk" high-frequency action and does it thoroughly — not a platform play. The
judgment behind it is clear: remote collaboration never solved pointing well, and
the problem was that tools were too heavy, not that there were too few features._

## What it actually does

- **Live annotation** → pen, highlighter, arrows, shapes, movable text, eraser,
  blur, and spotlight overlaid on the live screen
- **Whiteboard/blackboard overlay** → replace the desktop entirely with a board to
  sketch on; freeze the current frame while work continues behind it
- **Zoom and focus** → whole-screen zoom, a cursor-following lens, and a
  pointer-centered mode so the audience can follow details
- **Region capture** → snip an annotated region to clipboard or disk
- **Recording** → MP4 output mixing system and microphone audio, annotations baked
  into the result
- **Phone remote** → a free iPhone companion: trackpad plus gyroscope air-mouse,
  tool and color selection, highlight/flashlight toggle, zoom and freeze, remote
  drawing, undo and clear — paired over a direct local connection, no account

**Local-first**: recordings, screenshots, and annotations are saved to the Mac;
nothing uploads, purchases go through the App Store. Multi-monitor and Stage
Manager are supported.

## What old behavior it replaces

"Pointing at the screen while you explain" used to be solved by compromise:

Sharing the screen and moving the mouse cursor — small, jittery, and the audience
cannot tell which pixel you mean. Pre-drawing points into slides or screenshots —
only covers what you anticipated; nothing for on-the-fly insights. Using the video
conference's built-in annotation — one set per platform, minimal, and only works
inside that meeting window; switch to an IDE, browser, or terminal and it stops.
Or annotating a screenshot after the fact — but "annotate live" and "annotate
afterward" are two completely different actions.

ScreenMark replaces the "live, cross-app, no-interruption" slice of those
approaches: it floats as a layer above the screen, lets you draw over anything you
are sharing, and adds back the physical freedom of walking around with a phone as
your remote.

## Business model

**Freemium.** Free tier covers the core annotation tools. Pro unlocks recording,
live zoom, cursor highlight and flashlight, advanced annotation, custom break mode
and shortcuts, and the phone remote. Pricing: $4.99/month, $19.99/year, or $29.99
lifetime, with a 7-day trial.

_Read: a $30 buyout utility with restrained pricing, aimed at high-frequency
teaching/presenting users rather than the mass market. The ceiling is a niche
paying audience inside the Mac ecosystem, but the lifetime-license cash flow is
clean and free of renewal pressure._

## The call

**This is the "do a light tool properly" route, not the "build a platform" route.**

Everyone has the "point at something while explaining" pain in remote work, and
most solutions die from being too heavy: full whiteboard apps require pre-meeting
setup, a UI to learn, and content to migrate. ScreenMark's tradeoff is floating
over the existing screen, zero preparation, one-action trigger — it does not
replace anything you are already sharing, it just adds a transparent, drawable,
pointable layer.

**The transferable rule: a light tool's moat is habit, not features.** Once a
teacher gets used to "sketch it right there," they never go back to pre-drawing.
The phone remote adds back the physical freedom of "walking the room without
touching the keyboard," turning the tool from a desktop app into a teaching
instrument.

**Two limits**: it is macOS-bound and requires macOS 15 / M1 or newer, which shuts
out a large share of Intel Mac users (the official site and the App Store listing
disagree on requirements — worth verifying on your own machine); and there is no
exclusive feature moat — an open-source project or an Apple-native upgrade could
cover the same ground. The moat is mostly habit and the remote experience.

## What you can take from it

**Product logic**: for high-frequency micro-action tools, minimize the trigger cost
— live in the menu bar, zero setup, float over what the user is already using,
rather than making them migrate to a new interface. Users will not pay to "open
another app," but they will pay to "not interrupt what I'm doing."

**Positioning language**: nothing special to copy; its own line ("menu bar app for
live annotations, whiteboard overlay + more") is the model — describe the product
by the action, not by an abstract concept.

**Pricing structure**: free core + Pro buyout ($19.99/year or $29.99 lifetime) is
worth copying — for high-frequency small tools, a lifetime buyout triggers
"cheap enough to just try" far more easily than a monthly subscription.

## Verdict

**Worth watching.** A single point done well, restrained pricing, local-first —
a good sample of an independent developer building a light tool. But it is
macOS-bound with no exclusive moat, so the ceiling is limited. Its value is the
"how to turn a high-frequency micro-action into a habit product" thinking, more
than the market size.
