## What it is in one line

An unofficial clone of a commercial deck-generation feature. It reached 1,600 stars, then was wiped for copyright.

## Where it stands now: the repo is empty

The README is down to two sentences:

> This repository has been cleared due to copyright reasons.

The code is gone; the stars remain. **The value of this write-up isn't the product. It's how it died.**

## What it used to do

Reconstructed from the pre-takedown description:

- **A slides skill for AI agents** → let coding agents like Claude Code and Codex produce presentations
- **Editable PPTX output** → not an image or a PDF export, a file you can keep editing in PowerPoint
- **A bundled local browser editor** → preview and adjust the result locally after generation

**What it deliberately did not do**: no template library, no online collaboration.
It solved one thing: letting an agent produce an editable deck.

## What old behavior it replaces

Making a deck with AI actually goes: have the model write the outline, copy it into PowerPoint yourself, lay it out by hand, and then go back and redo it when the structure turns out wrong. The copying and the layout are pure manual labor.

That labor is what it replaced. **The old behavior is clear and the pain is real** —
"editable" is the whole point, because AI output you can't change is unusable in real work.

## The call

The thing worth remembering here: **it validated the demand, then was killed by its own positioning.**

1,600 stars says "let an agent produce an editable deck" is a real need — a harder number than any
set of user interviews. But it put a commercial product's brand in its name and the word "unofficial"
in its description, which is volunteering to be the target of a complaint. The same functionality
under a different name, claiming to replicate nobody, would very likely still be alive.

**The transferable rule: when you build on someone else's brand, your ceiling isn't your capability, it's their tolerance.** That tolerance can go to zero on any given day, and it doesn't need a reason.

The cost was total: not a fine, not a rename — every line of code deleted.
1,600 stars became a headstone. They still exist; what they point at doesn't.

This is a signal for the whole agent-skill ecosystem. A large share of skills today are built around
one specific product — replicating its capability, extending it, or routing around its limits.
**The legal standing of that whole class had never been tested. Now there's one result.**

## What to watch next

1. **Whether someone reopens it under a neutral name** — the same functionality surviving under a different name would prove the problem was the naming, not the feature
2. **Whether the original vendor ships it themselves** — a complaint is often followed by an official version, which would confirm the demand was validated for them too
3. **Whether other clone-style skills disappear too** — that's what separates an isolated case from a turning point

## What you can take from it

If you build skills or plugins around an existing product, this case hands you three specific rules — and the third one is the expensive one to learn yourself.

- **Don't put someone else's brand in your product name.** Adding "unofficial" doesn't help; it's an admission
- **Don't claim in your description that you replicate or are compatible with a named product.**
  Describe the problem you solve, not who you resemble
- **1,600 stars will not protect you.** User count isn't a moat; legal capacity is

**The opening this leaves**: the demand is now validated and the position is vacant.
A project that touches nobody's brand and simply says "let agents produce editable presentations"
has a good moment to walk in.

## Verdict

**Unproven.** The product no longer exists, but what it left behind is more useful than most
living products. Kept in the library as a case, not as a product.
