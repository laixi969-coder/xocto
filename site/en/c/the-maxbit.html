Inventing A Powered Garden Tool And Growing to $10K/Month - Starter

Real demand

Michele Morton sells a patented drill attachment that digs clean, flat-bottomed planting holes. Gardeners buy it at state trade shows and through wholesale accounts: a little over $13,000 in sales the first full year, past $60,000 by the end of Q2 in year two.

Real-demand verdict

Real-demand verdict

Real demand

It replaces a clear old behavior: hand-digging holes with shovels and trowels and redoing them repeatedly. The first 100 units sold out at a trade show, wholesale kept ordering, and sales grew in year two — people pay for it more than once.

How it makes money

One-time sales of a physical tool through trade-show retail and wholesale store accounts, no subscription. Early funding came as friend-and-family loans of $10,000 plus $5,000 a year later, on three-year terms at 10% interest.

What old behavior it replaces

Hand-digging holes with shovels, trowels and auger bits, then re-digging to fix uneven bottoms.

02

Where the first customers came from

The first 100-unit, single-size run was test-sold at a state trade show, where local gardeners bought out the entire batch.

03

Tactics you can copy

  1. 01Build only 100 units in a single size and sell them in person at a local trade show; treat selling out as validation before committing to more production.
  2. 02Turn friend-and-family funding into a formal event: invitations, a catered room, an outside speaker, and an envelope per guest for the investment form and check — over $10,000 raised in one sitting.
  3. 03Draft the patent yourself with a self-help book and the local Patent and Trademark Library, pay an attorney under $1,000 to finish it, and file a provisional first to hold your place before any public demo.
Moving it to an AI business

Not an AI business, but the sequence transfers: before pouring resources into building, sell the first 100 paid units in one vertical community or venue — you are validating willingness to pay, not demo applause.
