60-second business read

What it is in one line

A pixel-art solar system rendered at its real current positions — computed live from published orbital elements rather than copied from a list. Zoomable, browsable across 171 catalogued bodies, with six months of sky events ahead — and every body is also for sale: an asteroid costs $5, owned forever, with the explicit disclaimer that the name shows only on AstraPixels and is not an IAU designation.

Who built it

Built by the operator of Nick Launches (nicklaunches.com), a personal brand famous for weekly product launches and a tracker of 169 launch directories. AstraPixels was his own product, launched first at launch.

Read: this is "a person who understands distribution builds a product for himself." The author spends his week dissecting how other products launch, and when he built his own, he designed the launch logic directly into the product. That makes the commercial design more worth studying here than the astronomy.

What it actually does

  • A live pixel-art solar system → positions computed from published orbital elements, not copied from a list; "the map you open is the sky you are standing under"
  • Time travel → wind forward a week to watch movement; flip to true scale to see how much the map is lying to you
  • 171 catalogued objects → each with sourced facts and its current position
  • Sky events page → computes the next six months of conjunctions, oppositions, meteor showers, and eclipses
  • Buying bodies → asteroids $5 once, kept forever; moons $49; the Sun and comets are leased; the main belt's 3,000 procedurally generated rocks provide a $5 floor that can never sell out
  • Anti-link-rot → every external link checked monthly; three failures turn a body into a visibly decaying derelict sprite that recycles back into inventory

What it deliberately does not do: pretend to be an official designation. Every page showing a custom name carries the note that it is not an IAU name, is not submitted to anyone, and no observatory will recognize it. No sign-up — Stripe asks for your email and that is the whole account system.

What old behavior it replaces

This is essentially a modern re-run of the Million Dollar Homepage (2005) — a page that sold pixels. It replaces old behavior on two layers.

On the consumer side: to see "what the solar system looks like right now," you previously installed professional software like Stellarium (heavy, has a learning curve) or looked at a static diagram (positions fake and stale). AstraPixels makes it open-a-page and look, with positions that are real.

On the commercial side: it replaces the "sell virtual space" model itself. In the launch write-up the author explicitly broke down the Million Dollar Homepage's three death causes — inventory sells out or never does, users never return, links rot — and designed against each: 3,000 procedural rocks so inventory can never sell out; an astronomy tool first so there is a reason to return; monthly link checks so nothing rots.

Business model

Direct digital-asset sales: asteroids $5 forever, moons $49, scarce items (the Sun, the eight planet skins, comets) leased. No subscription, no sign-up; Stripe email is the account.

Read: day one brought five payments totaling $119 — a real revenue signal. But the way it failed is the lesson: all five buyers bought real catalogued bodies (the Moon at $49, Geographos at $25), none bought the $5 procedural rocks. The author wrote the lesson himself: scarcity was the product, and he had spent months engineering it away.

The call

A "useless beauty plus smart commercial design" combination; the commercial experiment is worth more attention than the features.

At bottom it is a distribution tool: the pixel solar system is "useless but beautiful," and beautiful things carry their own sharing motivation. The author understands this better than most, because his job is studying what spreads.

The genuinely valuable thing is its failure data. Five sales on day one, all real catalogued bodies, none of the $5 procedural rocks — the data directly contradicts the "never-sells-out" design. The author admits it: "Scarcity was the product, and I had spent months engineering it away." This is the most honest and most transferable commercial lesson in this batch: when you offer 3,000 homogeneous cheap items and 171 real bodies with names and stories, users buy the latter.

The positioning bet — "both toy and instrument" — is a risk. The author asks users in the launch post: "tell me which it looked like in your first five seconds." Until that question is answered, the retention story (the "nobody returns" death cause) isn't proven solved. The product needs users returning to see today's sky, not just to check on the rock they bought.

The transferable rule: design a reason to come back into a one-time purchase. The Million Dollar Homepage died from having no reason to return; AstraPixels makes "the sky is really different each time" that reason, which is what makes it smarter than the original. This design — make every visit slightly different — transfers to any digital product that otherwise sells once and is done.

What to watch next

① Whether return visits and repeat purchases appear in three months — whether the "nobody returns" death cause is actually solved by the live sky ② Whether scarcity pricing adjusts — real bodies priced up with demand, or the procedural rocks discounted or discontinued ③ Whether body claims reach triple digits — 5 on day one is a signal, not yet a sustainable business

What you can take from it

Product logic: for a "sells once" digital product, build in a mechanism that changes content every visit — AstraPixels uses live orbital positions; yours can be live data, live rankings, live content. Making each visit different is the general solution to "bought once, never returns."

Positioning language: "A name here is shown on AstraPixels and nowhere else. It is not an IAU designation." — a single negative confession builds trust instead of a buried disclaimer. That kind of upfront boundary-stating has its own shareability.

Pricing structure: the two-layer structure — leasing scarce items plus permanently selling many homogeneous cheap ones — is worth dissecting. But day-one data already proved the homogeneous-cheap layer was priced wrong. The corrected version: give scarcity back to scarce things, let real bodies' prices reflect demand.

Verdict

Worth watching. As a product it is a pretty toy; as a commercial experiment it offers the most honest lesson in this batch — scarcity is the product. PH #2 of the day, real day-one revenue, and an author willing to publish his own failed judgment add up to something worth recording. In three months, check returns and claim volume.
