What it is in one line

An MPC-style drum machine that runs in a browser tab: 16 velocity-sensitive pads across four banks, a 16-step sequencer with per-step velocity and swing, four patterns that chain into a song, and a sampler that chops any audio file or mic recording onto the pads. Every sound is synthesized in the browser — no install, no signup.

Who built it

Solo author Max Berggren, self-hunted at launch (launched 2026-08-07).

Read: the stated motivation — "I wanted a drum machine I could open in a tab and start playing straight away" — is classic self-driven building. Browser music tools are a stable Product Hunt category, and there is no company, team, or business-model information here.

What it actually does

  • 16 velocity-sensitive pads × 4 banks → MPC-style layout; press Space to start the loop, hit Z/C/A and you're making something in about a minute
  • 16-step sequencer → per-step velocity and adjustable swing
  • Four patterns chain into a song → structure, not just a single loop
  • Sampler → accepts any audio file or mic recording, finds the transients, and lays the chops across the pads
  • Everything synthesized in the browser → no sample library to download, nothing to wait for
  • Export → WAV, MIDI, or a link that rebuilds your beat exactly as you left it

What old behavior it replaces

Making music used to sit behind two paths, both with barriers. One: install a DAW (Ableton, FL Studio) plus sample libraries — expensive legally, risky cracked, and at least half an hour of setup before your first sound. Two: hardware drum machines (an actual MPC) — expensive, physical, needing an audio interface.

BAP Studio cuts the entire "before you start" stretch: open a tab and you're playing, with sound in the first minute. It doesn't replace a DAW's depth (arrangement, mixing, effect chains); it replaces the "I just want a beat quickly" scenario, whose previous lowest-cost option was a free drum-pad app on a phone — which still required finding and installing it on another device. A browser tab's zero-install is one step shorter than an app store download.

Read: it doesn't displace pro tools; it removes friction from impulsive creation. What this category really competes on is the first few seconds before the user acts, and the winner is the one who gets startup cost to zero.

Business model

Not disclosed. Free, no download, no signup; the page shows no trace of pricing or a paid tier.

Read: like most browser toys, this is "build it and let people use it." Absence of a model is not a flaw in itself — but understand how this category usually survives: donations, a later Pro layer, or simply as portfolio and personal brand for the author. None of the three is visible yet.

The call

A well-executed, simply-insighted browser tool with no business path yet; "unproven" because the evidence is too thin.

It nails zero-install: open and play, all synthesis in-browser, no sample downloads. For the "quick beat" scenario it is genuinely one of the smoothest entry points available.

But three problems stand. One, the category is crowded — browser drum machines are PH regulars, and without a unique hook users wash away with the tide. Two, there is no retention mechanism: this category's biggest threat is "play once, close the tab" — no account, no saved state (except manually exported links), no reason to return. Three, no business model: 80 upvotes is the heat of one day, not a business.

The design worth recording is the rebuild-link export: a link that reconstructs your beat exactly as you left it. This is music's "shareable save file" — the right instinct for turning a work into an acquisition channel, because a shared beat carries the product's address with it.

The transferable rule: for zero-install tools, the correct growth shape is "sharing is acquisition." When a product has no signup and no download, the only mechanism that brings new users is turning the user's output into "output that carries the product's address" — every share is a reopen. BAP Studio's rebuild link is the right prototype of that idea.

What to watch next

① Whether users actually share the generated links and whether those links stay open long-term — the share chain is this product's only growth engine ② Whether features or a Pro layer appear in three months — no movement means the author hasn't found a direction either ③ Whether organic traffic appears beyond PH heat (search, music forums, tutorials) — a single day of listing traffic doesn't sustain a product

What you can take from it

Product logic: when building an open-and-play tool, design sharing into the output itself — make every artifact users produce carry the product's entry point (rebuild link, watermark, replayable embed). Every share becomes acquisition, with no marketing spend.

Positioning language: "No install, no account, no sample library to download first." — three "no's" in a row say "startup cost is zero" in one line, stronger than a feature list.

Pricing structure: none. Not disclosed.

Verdict

Unproven. Solidly built and zero-install done right, but a crowded category, no retention mechanism, and no business model sit right in front of it; 80 upvotes don't carry a judgment. The metric worth following is not features but the share chain — if the rebuild links actually spread, this product has life; if not in three months, it's another fun browser toy.
