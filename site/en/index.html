Real-revenue case

Prayer Bowls: How Tom Karen Berry Built a Successful Christian Brand

Founder interview, self-reported

The habit of keeping prayer intentions on scraps of paper or homemade containers, and generic religious gifts as the default Christian gift choice.

What it is

A husband-and-wife team in Florida sells ceramic prayer bowls to Christian women 35 and older, splitting revenue roughly 50/50 between their own online store and wholesale to about 450 brick-and-mortar shops, at a self-reported $30K per month as of 2018.

Real-demand verdict

Real demand · The behavior it replaces is clear: keeping written prayer intentions for daily devotion, turned into a physical ritual object positioned as a gift. The founders note gift recipients immediately buy again to gift others, and two profitable years plus $30K/month suggest durable repeat demand, not a one-off spike.

Real-revenue case

These Two Founders Started An On-Demand Clothing Alterations Startup -

Founder interview, self-reported

Replaces spending weekends trekking to a tailor or dry cleaner, and removes the trust problem of paying for alterations you can't vet (the founders note dry cleaners aren't on Yelp).

What it is

ALTRD was a New York on-demand alterations service: customers ordered online, couriers handled pickup and delivery, and 25 home-based tailors did the sewing. Founders self-reported about $1,000 a month in revenue before shutting down in 2024.

Real-demand verdict

Nice-to-have · Alterations are already a paid habit — the founders cite dry cleaners charging $30 for a pant hem — and pilot users ordered repeatedly. But it's low-frequency convenience spending; the founders admitted they never found consistent customers, and the business shut down in 2024 without scaling.

Real-revenue case

How Regina Ye Started A CPG Company While In College

Founder interview, self-reported

The old habit of packing liquids in leak-prone travel bottles and generic toiletry bags.

What it is

A college student built ZIRUI, a modular magnetic travel toiletry case, pre-sold it on Kickstarter to fund tooling and the first production run, and now sells direct through Shopify, self-reporting about $1,000 a month in revenue.

Real-demand verdict

Real demand · It replaces a concrete old habit: packing liquids in generic bags and bottles that leak. Strangers described the pain unprompted, Kickstarter backers paid up front, and the store keeps selling, so demand shows up as money, not just enthusiasm, though the scale is still tiny.
