FUNDING DESK · Portugal · Sovereign, controllable AI agent cloud infrastructure (Agentic Cloud), delivered both as a networked compute service and as customer-owned modular container data centres.

Granite

Granite is building an "Agentic Cloud" for AI agents, pitching customer-held keys, node-level encryption and modular data centres across a six-layer stack from bare metal and GPUs to storage, networking, compute and an agent runtime.

Work it replaces

It replaces the common pattern of stitching together GPU instances, object storage and container orchestration on a hyperscaler and then layering an agent framework on top; it also partly replaces enterprise-owned data centres combined with third-party cloud management.

Business judgment

The key commercial bet is that agentic workloads need sovereign, purpose-built infrastructure rather than an orchestration layer on top of general-purpose clouds; the available evidence proves architectural claims and self-reported network size, not paid demand or unit economics.

01

What the product does

The site describes six layers: Metal (bare-metal servers, routers and power managed as one fleet), GPU (owned and rented GPUs, inference engines and an AI broker), Storage (content-addressed filesystem, S3/Redis-compatible object storage, deduplication and quantum-safe storage in beta), Network (the Mycelium overlay with DNS, gateway, NAT64 and GeoIP), Compute (Mycelium OS slicing machines into VMs, containers or bare metal), and Agentic (Hero OS with orchestration, memory and objects). There are two delivery modes: Granite Web Services for consuming compute, storage, GPUs and agents over the network, and Granite Infrastructure, a liquid-cooled container unit owned by the customer and deployed at a site of their choosing. The site says it is model-agnostic and that value sits in Hero OS, the agent runtime. All of this is company-reported; the product architecture document requires an email, and APIs, SLAs and limits are not public.

02

Users, buyers and demand

The inferred users are teams that need agents to run inside a controlled boundary: enterprise IT and security owners with hard constraints on data residency, key custody and jurisdiction, plus AI application teams needing substantial GPU capacity without long-term lock-in to one cloud. The payer is more likely an infrastructure or platform engineering function carrying compliance and procurement responsibility than an individual developer. Pain frequency depends on workload persistence and data sensitivity: experimental API calls are easier on a general cloud, while continuous inference over private data with audit requirements makes control a recurring problem. No named customers, industry mix or buying process are disclosed, so this remains a conditional inference.

03

The actual workflow

There are two paths from input to delivery. On Web Services, a customer specifies compute, storage, GPU and agent needs, reserves nodes or slices on the network, connects their own models to Hero OS, and the orchestrator allocates memory and object storage, with production in days; data stays at the chosen location and keys stay with the customer. On Infrastructure, the customer prepares a pad, power and cooling, Granite delivers a self-contained liquid-cooled container, and the site says contract-to-production takes four to nine months; once connected, the unit joins the same network. Both paths share the stateless Mycelium OS layer, where a failed node reboots from a small image and rejoins automatically. Billing granularity, quota approval, migration tooling and support response processes are not described, and these must be filled in before judging the real workflow.

04

Pricing and unit economics

No prices, billing units or contract terms are published; the site only gives slice granularity (from one core to 64) and two consumption modes: on-demand network capacity, or buying and owning a hardware unit. The inferable revenue structure is a hybrid of usage-based cloud services plus hardware delivery and deployment, but this is inference, not disclosure. Unit economics hinge on whether modular units' capex and four-to-nine-month delivery cycles can be offset by high utilisation, and on how revenue is shared with the 750-plus independent node operators. The site stresses no egress lock-in, meaning revenue cannot rely on migration penalties and must come from sustained usage or hardware margin. Pricing pages, contract terms, minimum commitments and GPU premiums are missing, so gross margin structure cannot be assessed.

05

Adoption evidence and gaps

Company-reported network figures are 750-plus independent nodes, 40-plus countries, 10,896 CPU cores and 1,196,055 GB of storage online, with a single container able to hold 80 GPUs (ten GPU nodes of eight each), 5,760 TB of storage and 3,000 agents. The site says the platform is live and in deployment, with the first Infrastructure unit shipping in a month of 2026 that appears as a placeholder in the captured text. These are self-reported, unaudited and not evidence of paid adoption. Missing evidence includes paying customer counts and retention, actual utilisation, revenue, per-node economics, SLA attainment and any third-party performance benchmark. The funding record itself only shows financial backing, not demand validation.

06

Competition and defensibility

Versus general models or general clouds, Granite's difference is not model capability but architectural commitments: node-level encryption with customer-held keys, a stateless operating layer for self-healing, hardware agnosticism to avoid single-vendor lock-in, and treating energy and data-centre siting as first-class design variables. If independently verified, these create switching costs and a compliance narrative. The moat holds only if Hero OS orchestration genuinely beats open-source agent frameworks, network scale translates into stable performance, and decentralised operation by independent node owners does not sacrifice observability and support quality. If these remain marketing language, customers can return to a general cloud plus their own key management and the moat disappears quickly.

07

How to read this round

This round is EUR 4 million, labelled Other, with Bifrost Studios as investor, dated 28 September 2026. That size fits an early-stage infrastructure company: enough to fund team growth and delivery of a first container unit, but far short of building large data centres, so it more plausibly goes to software, network operations and initial deployments. Use of funds is undisclosed and should not be inferred. The next milestones are on-time delivery and network connection of the first Infrastructure unit, verifiable paid usage on Web Services, and publicly reproducible performance and security validation. Because the round is Other, it should not be read as Seed or Series A.

08

Where it could fail

First, delivery risk: modular data centres depend on customer site, power and cooling readiness, and a four-to-nine-month cycle slipping would hit both revenue recognition and reputation. Second, demand risk: if agent workloads end up mostly on managed services atop general clouds, the sovereign-infrastructure premium may not hold and self-reported network scale will not convert into payment. Third, operational risk: 750-plus independent node operators disperse quality, compliance and security responsibility, and any compromised node or data breach would undercut the core promise that keys stay with the customer. Fourth, capital risk: hardware delivery and network expansion are cash-hungry, and EUR 4 million leaves limited room for error in an asset-heavy model.

09

What you can take from it

The transferable idea is making control an architectural property rather than a contract clause: key custody, data location and no-egress-lock-in are more persuasive when technically enforced than when written into an SLA. Offering both "own the hardware" and "consume the network" entry points also lowers procurement friction by letting customers start small and migrate to their own deployment. The counter-lesson is that architectural narrative must be paired with verifiable pricing, customer cases and performance data, or even a well-drawn six-layer stack will struggle to close sales.

10

What to watch next

Verifiable next indicators: whether the first Infrastructure unit ships within the promised window with public evidence of network connection; whether Web Services publishes pricing and minimum commitments; whether named paying customers or reproducible third-party benchmarks appear; whether node counts and online cores keep growing rather than being a one-off disclosure; and whether Hero OS opens documentation and APIs for external testing. If these remain website claims, Granite should be treated as an early project whose architecture is not yet market-validated.
