Work it replaces

Business judgment

Palma.ai addresses the critical bottleneck of governance and compliance in scaling enterprise AI agents; its commercial value depends on embedding the governance layer into existing enterprise identity and infrastructure, and proving its indispensability beyond generic models and MCP gateways.

01

What the product does

Palma.ai offers an enterprise governance layer that sits between AI agents and tools, providing identity, entitlement, policy enforcement, human approval, and audit capabilities. It supports all major AI assistants (e.g., Claude, ChatGPT, Copilot, Cursor) and MCP servers, delivering tools and Skills through a single connector. The platform emphasizes parameter-level policies, PII redaction, tamper-evident audit, and FinOps cost tracking, and can be deployed on-prem, in VPC, or air-gapped. The website also describes Skills as versioned, scanned playbooks that distribute best practices to all agents.

02

Users, buyers and demand

Target customers are large enterprises with significant AI agent usage, particularly IT security, compliance, and operations teams. Payers are likely CISOs, CIOs, or platform engineering leads, with pain points around lack of visibility, uncontrolled permissions, audit difficulties, and compliance risks. The website does not disclose specific customer cases or industry distribution, but mentions scale from 'ten to a hundred thousand users,' suggesting mid-to-large enterprises.

03

The actual workflow

The workflow starts with syncing user groups from existing identity providers (e.g., Entra, Okta). Palma assigns each user a governed connector that carries the MCP servers and Skills they are entitled to. When a user initiates a tool call through any AI assistant, the request passes through Palma's policy layer: identity verification, parameter-level policy checks, pausing for human approval if needed, redacting sensitive data, and logging a complete audit trail (including caller, agent, arguments, result, cost, and duration). Audit logs are tamper-evident, verifiable offline, and stream to SIEM. Skills, as versioned playbooks, are scanned and distributed through the same connector.

04

Pricing and unit economics

The website does not disclose specific pricing or charging models. Based on the product positioning, it may use enterprise subscriptions or charge per connector/user, but there is no evidence. Unit economics inference: if charging per user, deployment costs (on-prem/VPC) and governance complexity must be considered; if charging per call volume, balancing audit storage and compute costs is necessary. The revenue model remains unconfirmed pending official disclosure.

05

Adoption evidence and gaps

The website mentions scale from 'ten to a hundred thousand users' and shows interface screenshots (e.g., tool call statistics, policy status), but does not provide specific customer names, numbers, or revenue data. Investors include D11Z Ventures, Plug and Play, Deel, among others. Partners from D11Z and Plug and Play endorse as customers, stating 'we are not just investors, we run it ourselves.' This provides early adoption signals but lacks independently verified customer cases or retention data.

06

Competition and defensibility

Palma.ai's moat lies in its positioning as an enterprise governance layer: it is not tied to a specific AI assistant or model, but based on the open MCP standard and deeply integrated with enterprise identity systems (Entra, Okta) and infrastructure (on-prem, VPC, air-gapped). Compared to generic model safety features, Palma offers parameter-level policies, human approval, and tamper-evident audit—hard requirements for enterprise compliance. However, the moat's sustainability depends on the fragmentation of the MCP ecosystem and whether large cloud providers launch similar governance layers. If MCP standards are widely adopted and governance needs become standardized, Palma may face competition from platform players.

07

How to read this round

This is a pre-seed round of $1.8 million (approximately €1.577 million at ECB rate), with investors including Scale Now Ventures, D11Z Ventures, Deel Ventures, and Plug and Play Ventures. This stage indicates the company is in early product validation and market entry, with funds likely for product development, team building, and early customer acquisition. However, the use of funds is undisclosed, so specific allocation cannot be confirmed. The next milestone may be acquiring paying enterprise customers, validating product-market fit, and establishing a repeatable sales process.

08

Where it could fail

First, enterprise governance needs may be met by cloud providers or built-in AI assistant features, compressing Palma's independent value. Second, if the MCP standard does not become mainstream in enterprises, Palma's ecosystem bet may fail. Third, early customer acquisition relies on investor endorsement; if it cannot convert to independent paying customers, growth will be limited. Fourth, deployment models (on-prem/VPC) may bring high implementation costs, affecting unit economics.

09

What you can take from it

Palma.ai's case shows that during the AI agent explosion, governance and security may become key bottlenecks for enterprise adoption. Entrepreneurs can learn from its strategy of 'not binding to models, based on open standards, embedding into existing identity systems,' and using investors as early customers for feedback and trust. At the same time, be wary of platform competition and standard uncertainty.

10

What to watch next

Watch whether Palma.ai discloses paying customer numbers, revenue, or specific case studies; observe the depth of integration with major AI assistants and MCP servers; monitor the adoption progress of the MCP standard and whether cloud providers launch competitive governance products.
