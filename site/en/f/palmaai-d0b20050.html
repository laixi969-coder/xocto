Palma.ai

Palma.ai offers enterprises a central governance layer for MCP tools and Skills, letting employees use identity-, policy- and audit-constrained agent capabilities inside whichever AI assistant they already open.

Work it replaces

It substitutes for per-team MCP gateways, scattered API keys and permission configs, manual approval emails, and logs stitched together after the fact for audits.

Business judgment

The key commercial bet is that the enterprise bottleneck for agents is shifting from model capability to permissions, approvals and auditability, and that this governance layer will persist as neutral infrastructure across assistants rather than being absorbed by a single model vendor.

01

What the product does

Palma positions a policy layer between every agent and every tool: one connector delivers MCP servers and Skills, assigned by identity-provider group, into Claude, ChatGPT, Copilot, Cursor and other clients. It claims to inspect the actual arguments of a tool call, pause high-risk actions for a named approver, redact sensitive fields, and produce a tamper-evident audit record verifiable offline with the customer's own key. It also offers a unified catalog across Microsoft, Google and AWS registries plus usage and cost views by agent, team and business unit. All of this is company-reported and not independently verified.

02

Users, buyers and demand

Users are business and engineering staff granted agent access; the payer is more likely IT, security or platform engineering, since the site explicitly sells letting IT say yes to agents doing real work. Pain frequency scales with call volume: credential and permission management is a daily burden, while approvals and audits spike around compliance reviews, incident postmortems and month-end close. No public customer list or industry mix is available, so it is unclear whether buyers are large regulated enterprises or mid-sized tech companies.

03

The actual workflow

An admin assigns connectors through Entra or Okta IdP groups; an employee then receives entitled MCP tools and versioned Skills directly in the assistant they already use, with no per-user install or credentials on a laptop. When an agent issues a tool call, Palma reads the request arguments against policy, releases and records low-risk calls, routes high-risk ones to a named approver, and redacts sensitive data before it leaves the enterprise environment. Each call is attributed to the person, agent and application, with arguments, result, duration and cost streamed to the existing SIEM.

04

Pricing and unit economics

The site discloses no pricing, billing unit or contract structure, so it is impossible to tell whether Palma charges per seat, per call or per deployment. The disclosed deployment options include VPC, on-premises and fully air-gapped environments, optionally hosted by Palma; such delivery usually implies heavier implementation and operations effort and an enterprise-contract economics profile rather than self-serve subscription, but that is inference, not disclosure. The missing pricing page is a principal research gap.

05

Adoption evidence and gaps

Confirmed adoption signals are limited: investor testimonials from D11Z and Plug and Play say they are both investors and customers and actually run the product, and the site shows interface mockups of policies, call volumes and costs. These are company-reported and investor-endorsed, not evidence of revenue, retention or paid scale. With no public case studies, customer counts, ARR or renewal data, traction should be judged conservatively.

06

Competition and defensibility

Versus general model vendors, Palma's differentiation is cross-assistant neutrality and deep binding to the enterprise's existing identity system: rules live in the governance layer, so swapping assistants does not require reconfiguration. Versus self-built gateways, the moat depends on whether engineering details such as offline-verifiable audit, argument-level policy and versioned Skill distribution hold up in practice. The real moat condition is becoming the default governance standard in enterprise procurement and accumulating policy libraries and integration breadth; if model vendors bundle equivalent governance for free, or the MCP standard absorbs these functions, the neutral layer's value compresses.

07

How to read this round

This is a pre-seed round; amount and native currency are rendered by the deterministic layer, with investors including Scale Now Ventures, D11Z Ventures, Deel Ventures and Plug and Play Ventures. The list is not a lead designation, and use of funds is undisclosed. The stage suggests the product has moved from concept to a demonstrable enterprise deployment form and has at least one investor using it internally, but paid demand remains unproven. The next milestone should be publicly verifiable paying-customer cases, a pricing structure and security compliance certifications.

08

Where it could fail

First, if enterprise buyers demand SOC 2, ISO 27001 or lengthy security reviews that Palma cannot supply quickly, sales cycles may stretch beyond pre-seed runway. Second, if model vendors or the MCP ecosystem natively provide permissions and audit, the middle layer may be treated as an optional cost. Third, air-gapped and on-prem delivery, while attractive to large customers, may create high implementation cost and poor replicability, making revenue dependent on heavy human delivery. Fourth, policy misjudgment that blocks legitimate calls or fails to catch sensitive data would directly destroy trust.

09

What you can take from it

A transferable practice is to treat governance as the product rather than an add-on: distribute permissions via identity-provider groups, bind policy to call arguments rather than identity alone, and let customers verify audit records independently with their own keys. Another lesson is defining Skills as versioned, scanned enterprise process assets, turning a best operator's method into a productized capability.

10

What to watch next

Watch for a public pricing page and billing unit; named paying-customer cases and their deployment form; security compliance certifications; and third-party or customer-verified evidence that policy enforcement and audit verification work as claimed. These indicators say more about whether the governance layer is actually purchased than the funding amount does.
