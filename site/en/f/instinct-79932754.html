FUNDING DESK · United States · Consumer general-purpose task-execution AI assistant (device-operating agent)

Instinct

Instinct is a personal assistant reached by text or phone call that operates a phone and computer the way a user would, handling tasks such as ordering ingredients, booking train tickets, buying groceries, sending flowers, and finding a court.

Work it replaces

It replaces the user's own app-switching, comparing, form-filling, and checkout loops, and partially replaces human assistants and errand services.

Business judgment

The bet is not a smarter chatbot but an AI that takes over real devices and accounts to execute cross-app tasks; success hinges on execution reliability and authorization safety rather than model capability alone.

01

What the product does

Per the company site, the assistant understands what the user is working on and what matters to them, and uses a phone and computer the same way the user does. Listed examples include ordering ingredients for a new recipe, booking a train home for the weekend, ordering groceries, sending flowers to a friend, and finding a court for the weekend. That points to end-to-end execution across apps rather than a single vertical; however, the site does not state which apps, regions, or device platforms are supported, nor how retries and human handoff work, which remain research gaps.

02

Users, buyers and demand

Users are ordinary consumers who want to offload errands, and the payer is most likely the individual user, with the pain being frequent, low-value-per-task friction across many apps. The site discloses no target persona, geographic coverage, or customer case, so it is unclear whether early users are novelty seekers or people with recurring needs; no evidence of enterprise or family account structures was found.

03

The actual workflow

The flow is: the user describes a goal in natural language via text or voice, the assistant parses intent and constraints, then executes searches, selections, form fills, and submissions on phone and computer, and reports back. The site stresses there are no new interfaces, meaning the entry point reuses existing messaging channels and lowers learning cost; payment authorization, identity verification, exception handling, and user confirmation points are not described in the retrieved text and are key process gaps.

04

Pricing and unit economics

The input evidence contains no pricing page or fee description, so per-task, subscription, or commission models cannot be confirmed and the service must not be described as free. A subscription or a service fee on placed orders is a plausible but unverified direction; unit economics depend on human-intervention rate, payment failure rate, and third-party platform risk controls, none of which are disclosed.

05

Adoption evidence and gaps

The only verifiable progress is a large financing round with investors including Sequoia Capital, Benchmark, and Coatue; an investor list is not a lead-investor list. The site discloses no user counts, task volumes, retention, or merchant partnerships, so funding is not evidence of demand validation or product success.

06

Competition and defensibility

Versus general models, the potential moat lies in the device-operation layer, account authorization systems, reliability engineering for cross-app execution, and failure-recovery experience, not the model itself. Versus the old workflow, the advantage is compressing multi-step manual work into one conversation. That moat only holds if execution success clearly beats doing it yourself and if security and trust mechanisms justify handing over payment access; no public data supports this yet.

07

How to read this round

A Series C at this scale signals that investors are willing to make a large bet on execution-oriented agents, and that the company now needs to prove scalable reliability rather than demo quality. Use of proceeds is undisclosed; the next milestone should be publicly verifiable task-completion, retention, and security-incident metrics.

08

Where it could fail

First, execution errors or wrong actions (incorrect orders, duplicate payments) cause direct financial loss and destroy trust. Second, account and payment authorization creates serious security and compliance exposure, with regulatory and legal risk if breached or abused. Third, third-party platforms may block automated operation, making capabilities fragile. Fourth, if human intervention per task is costly, scale increases losses.

09

What you can take from it

For founders, the transferable idea is packaging AI as something that reuses the user's existing entry points and completes actions on their behalf, lowering adoption friction; but authorization, confirmation, and rollback mechanisms must be built in parallel, or trust costs will consume the experience gains.

10

What to watch next

Verifiable indicators to track: whether the site publishes a supported-app and region list, launches a pricing page, discloses task success and human-handoff rates, and whether real user cases or a security whitepaper appear after the round.
